### The four instruments

| Instrument | What the partner gets |
| --- | --- |
| **Flagship name** | The premium one-word registration — `airbnb.bnb`, `openai.gpt` — reserved and allocated at launch rather than sold at auction |
| **Right of first refusal** | First option on further names in the TLD, and on renewing the arrangement before it is offered to anyone else |
| **Co-marketing** | Joint launch, named as the partner that defines the TLD, and the announcement that goes with it |
| **Category distribution** | The exclusive right to sell the TLD inside their product category, for a defined term — a real exclusive, in the channel |

**Category distribution is the one that is genuinely exclusive**, and it is the
most valuable of the four. Apple can be the only podcast platform authorised to
sell `.pod` inside a podcast application. That is an ordinary commercial
contract between two companies, scoped to a category and a term — the same
instrument the rest of this proposal is built on, pointed at a flagship brand
instead of a mid-size platform.

### Why this shape rather than handing over the TLD

Three reasons, and none of them are concessions.

1. **The TLD keeps earning.** A TLD handed to one company sells once. An
   open `.bnb` with Airbnb as its flagship sells to every host, property
   manager and rental brand — while Airbnb still gets the name and the
   announcement it actually wanted.
2. **Multiple claimants stop being a problem.** `.view` has Figma and Adobe,
   `.coin` has Binance and Coinbase, `.pod` has Apple and Spotify. Handing the
   TLD to one forecloses the other's revenue. Category-scoped distribution
   does not: Figma takes design tools, Adobe takes creative suites, on the same
   TLD.
3. **It is a faster signature.** Distribution rights in one category is a
   partnerships decision, made by people who make partnerships decisions every
   quarter. Transferring control of a namespace is a legal and regulatory
   project with a different set of approvers.

There is also a rule underneath it worth knowing once: a registry cannot hand a
generic TLD to a single company as a private namespace — ICANN closed that
door for this round in January 2024 — and a trademarked TLD has to be applied
for by its owner. Neither constraint touches any of the four instruments above,
which is why the package is built from them.

`.gpt` is unusually clean on this front. OpenAI applied to register "GPT" and
the USPTO **finally refused it on 6 February 2024** as merely descriptive and
used industry-wide. [^2958d7] [^de6a4f] There is no mark to license and no basis
for an objection in either direction — LFG can operate the TLD, and OpenAI
can be its flagship partner, without anyone's lawyers being involved.
